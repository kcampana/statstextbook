12.1 Learning Objectives

  • Know what a chi-square goodness of fit test is, and what types of research questions it can address.
  • Know what a chi-square test of independence is, and what types of research questions it can address.
  • Know how to calculate effect sizes for chi-square tests.
  • Know how to write up the results of a chi-square test.

All the statistical tests that we’ve covered so far are parametric tests – that is, they are intended to predict a continuous outcome of some sort. I do find that students sometimes need to work with categorical data, so I think it’s worth the time to dip a toe into some non-parametric tests, namely the chi-square goodness of fit, and the chi-square test of independence.

12.2 The Chi Square Goodness of Fit Index

The chi-square goodness of fit index (GFI) pops up in a variety of places (for example, if you ever go on to do structural equation modeling, this is sometimes an indication of how well a model fits the data). For the purposes of this text, though, the chi-square GFI is helpful in examining whether participants are falling into categories in any predictable way.

For example, imagine that we want to learn about people’s preference for transportation among the options of car, bus, bike or walk. If there’s no strong preference for one of these options, we’d expect to see people equally distributed among the options. So, we will compare the proportion of people who report each preference with our null hypothesis of equal numbers of people in each group. Imagine we collect data from 100 people, and here’s what we find:

I should mention briefly that the null hypothesis does not have to assume that all groups are equal; for example, left-handers make up about 10% of the population, so if we wanted to see if a certain group deviates from this value (for example, American Presidents) we can set up our expected value to reflect the population proportions.

Returning to our transportation example, we see that there are some deviations from what we expected to see here – cars are more popular, and buses are less popular than our baseline expectation. The chi-square test will tell us whether these deviations are large enough that we can feel like they are not due to chance. The calculation is relatively straightforward:

\(\chi^2=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\frac{(obtained_{ij} - expected_{ij})^2}{expected_{ij}}}\)

Essentially, we take the difference between the expected and obtained values in each transportation type, square that value, and divide that value by the expected number. We do that for each group and sum up these values. In this data, we find the chi-square value to be 12.32. The degrees of freedom for this are the number of groups minus 1 (so, 3 in this case) and the critical value for the chi-square test for 3 degrees of freedom is 7.82 (you can see Table 16.1 for reference), so our finding is significant. The way we interpret this finding is a bit different from what you’re used to if you’ve been thinking about t-tests and ANOVAs. All this test tells us is that people are not distributing themselves equally among different transportation modes; we have to refer back to the table to interpret it by identifying where those deviations are. In this example, we can see that car is most preferred, and bus is least preferred. We also see that walking and biking are a bit above what we expected as well. However, there’s no post-hoc test or contrast needed for a chi-square; we can just report the table and point out where the biggest deviations are.

12.3 Chi-Square Test of Independence

A second type of useful non-parametric test is the chi-square test of independence (TOI). This differs from a chi-square GFI in that it requires two variables, and we’re looking to see whether those two variables are related to each other. In fact, you’ve already seen a version of this in Section 4.5, when I talked about hypothesis testing using a hypothetical example of children watching violent media.

Here, let’s walk through another slightly more complex example. Mays et al. (2014) did a study looking at how parental smoking behavior relates to youth smoking. I’m simplifying the data a bit, but generally they found three categories of parental smoking behavior (current smoker, former smoker, non-smoker) and four categories of youth smoking behaviors (never-smoker, early regular smoker, early experimental smoking, late experimental smoking). Here’s how the data looks:

Conveniently, the chi-square TOI uses the same equation as the chi-square GFI:

\(\chi^2=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\frac{(obtained_{ij} - expected_{ij})^2}{expected_{ij}}}\)

But the expected values are not as straightforward as they were before, because we don’t have nice, equal groups. As we learned in section Section 4.4, though, the multiplicative rule comes through for us here. We use the total rows/columns to help us calculate the probability someone is in a particular category, then use the multiplicative rule to calculate what each cell total should be if the two variables are independent of one another. If the obtained number deviates a lot from that expected value, we can conclude those variables are likely related.

Let’s take, for example, youth who are never-smokers and who had a non-smoker parent. The probability of being a never smoker is \(\frac{121}{400}=.30\) and the probability of having a non-smoker as a parent is \(\frac{107}{400}=.27\). So, according to the multiplicative rule, there is a \(.30 \times .27 = .08\) chance of someone being in the never-smoker, non-smoker cell. In a group of 400 people, that would be \(.08 \times 400=32\) people. When we compare this expected value of 32 to the obtained value of 16, we can see that there might indeed be a relationship. But, we’ll want to make sure so we conduct our chi-square test. The degrees of freedom for this are the number of rows, minus one, times the number of columns, minus one:

\((3-1)\times(4-1)=6\)

Ultimately, we end up with a value of 48.20 for our chi-square value, which is significant (see Table 16.1). Again, there is no need for post-hoc tests, we simply need to examine the table and interpret where participants are notably above or below the expected value. If we examine residual values, we see that the non-smoker/never-smoker category is much lower than expected, as is the current smoker/early experimenter group. Meanwhile, the current smoker/never-smoker group and the non-smoker/early experimental groups have more than we would have expected. So there is some indication that having a current smoker as a parent is associated with their child avoiding smoking, and that having a non-smoking parent might be associated with some early experimentation with smoking. This was a correlational study, so we can’t reasonably argue a causal link here, just an association between these categories.

12.4 Chi Square Assumptions

Non-parametric test have some different assumptions from the ones we’re used to seeing with parametric tests. Specifically, chi-square assumptions are:

  1. Independence: Chi-squares assume that each participant inhabits only one cell; we cannot use this test with a dependent/repeated measures design.

  2. Expected values should be at least 5 participants.

For Mays et al. (2014) study, both of these conditions are satisfied. Youth can only be in one category, and when they discussed parental behaviors, they had only one parent fill out the form. Although we can see that some of the obtained values in the table are under 5, none of the expected values were less than 5. In the event that you do have data that violates this assumption, the Fisher’s Exact Test can be used as an alternative test that is more forgiving of small expected values.

If you violate the assumption of independence, you need to find a more appropriate test to analyze your data.

12.5 Calculating the Effect Size of a Chi-Square Test

For a chi-square GFI, you can use a Cohen’s w, which is a straightforward calculation:

\(w = \sqrt{\frac{\chi^2}{N}}\)

So, for our example, we can use the formula to see we have a fairly moderate-sized effect:

\(w = \sqrt{\frac{\ 12.32}{100}}=.35\)

For a chi-square TOI, the effect size is known as \(\phi\) , which we will cover in section Section 13.3.1.

12.6 Writing the Results of a Chi-Square Test

When sharing the results of a chi-square test, you want to convey the following information to your audience:

  • What type of chi-square test you are doing;

  • What the obtained values are (often best conveyed in a table, as I have done in this chapter)

  • The chi-square test value, its degrees of freedom, and its significance;

  • In some cases, the effect size

So, for the study, I might provide a table of results and say something like:

A chi-square test of independence indicates a significant relationship between youth and parental smoking behaviors, \(\chi^2\)(6) = 48.20, p<.001, \(\phi\)=. As can be noted from Table 12.3, youth who have non-smoking parents are much more likely to do some early experimentation with smoking, whereas youth who have a parent who currently smokes are more likely to completely avoid smoking.

You will have the best sense of what particular cells hold the most interest for your audience, so use your best judgement for how to explain the relationship. It’s not always particularly straightforward!
