5  Hypothesis Testing

Author

Kristie Campana

5.1 Learning Objectives:

  • Understand the idea behind hypothesis testing, and how it bridges the gap between theory and data.

  • Know the difference between one- and two-tailed hypotheses (aka directional and non-directional hypotheses) and why it’s important to be clear about your hypotheses prior to starting an analysis.

  • Understand the possible outcomes of a hypothesis test (correct decision, Type I error, Type II error)

  • Know the four ways we can increase the power of a study.

  • Understand the difference between the frequentist approach (which uses null hypothesis significance testing) and Bayesian approach (which uses other approaches to defining “importance” of findings).

Hypothesis testing is incredibly important to the work we do, but I also think it is one of the most widely misunderstood aspects of social science… by students, by laypeople, and unfortunately, sometimes by scientists themselves. If you have some experience in research, you know there’s a typical cycle research follows:

  1. Identify a theory that seems interesting, or observe a phenomenon that seems interesting.

  2. Devise a hypothesis about that phenomenon you want to test.

  3. Decide how to measure (and/or manipulate) your variables.

  4. Conduct your test and collect data.

  5. If your data supports the existing theory, add it to the pile of evidence. If the data does not support the existing theory, consider reasons why that might be the case.

  6. Start the process over again with a new question.

Solid science is built on devising testable hypotheses based on theories, and then researching and replicating that research to build up a body of evidence. While this all seems simple, it is not! It is easy to get sloppy with this process. Researchers started to raise concerns about this back in the 1950s, but this all came to a head in the 2010s when it was discovered that only around 40% of psychological studies could be replicated (see Baker (2015)). There are several reasons we’re in this situation now:

  • Journals are not terribly excited about publishing articles that said “We did a study and found that none of our hypotheses worked.” So, studies that show a lack of support for a theory get thrown into a desk drawer and forgotten about.

  • Researchers are also not terribly excited to replicate other researchers’ studies. Social science and statistics are established on the assumption that we will test theories many times on different samples, but in practice, this doesn’t happen as much as it should. If we don’t do these replications, we never have an opportunity to refute the findings of a study with a new sample.

  • Many researchers are often required to publish their research in order to keep their jobs or their funding. This creates an incentive to game the system so research turns out a significant finding. This can range from questionable methods (p-hacking is one example of this, which I will talk about in more detail later in the next chapter) to outright falsification of data.

In this chapter, I’ll spend some time talking through what hypothesis testing is and how it works (or how it should work, anyway). But hypothesis testing tends to be a rather black-and-white way of thinking about a world that contains many shades of gray, so I’ll also talk about some alternative ways to think about hypotheses and how they relate to theories and results.

5.2 Why do we bother with hypothesis testing?

The best way to make a case for the importance of hypothesis testing comes from an old analogy known as the Texas Sharpshooter:

An army commander is marching his troops through the Texas countryside. They are exhausted and need to rest, and the commander spots a farm on the horizon. They stop at the farm and ask the farmer living there if they can take shelter overnight in his barn. The farmer agrees, so the troops go to the barn and get settled.

The commander walks around the side of the barn and is shocked to see a series of bulls-eyes painted on the side of the barn, with a bullet hole perfectly in the center of each bulls-eye. The commander rushes back to the farmhouse and asks the farmer “Who shot up the side of your barn? I need a sharpshooter for my troops!”

The farmer laughs and says “My son did the shooting, and trust me, you don’t want him.”

“Why not?” asks the commander.

“Because he shoots the barn first, and then draws the bulls-eyes around the bullet holes.”

I mentioned way back in Chapter 1 that humans are not very good at avoiding their biases. Because a lot of social science feels like “common sense,” it is easy to justify any finding after the fact. Imagine that I tell my students that I found a +.60 relationship between how much you like your coworkers and how good your job performance is. They might say “Of course, that makes perfect sense! If you are happy at work, you’ll feel motivated to do a good job.” Now imagine I say that actually, it’s a -.60 correlation between those variables. My students might say “Of course, that makes perfect sense! If you like your coworkers, you’ll spend time chatting with them instead of doing your work.” The point is, in social science, it’s easy to come up with explanations after the fact!

When we make up a story to fit our data, this is known as post-hoc theorizing and it is best to avoid this because it allows our biases to determine the story rather than the data. So, having a clear and testable hypothesis before you collect any data is really how science should work.

To have a testable hypothesis, we need to be clear about what our variables are, and what operational definition we are using for each variable. We should also be clear about whether we have an idea about the nature of the relationship. In some cases, there might be a theory that makes the expectation very clear, so we can devise a one-tailed hypothesis (also known as a directional hypothesis) where we can be specific about what we expect to find:

There will be a positive relationship between job satisfaction and job performance.

Children who watch violent media will be more likely to behave aggressively than children who do not watch violent media

In some cases, we might not be sure, or there might be competing theories and we want to see which one bears out in a particular situation. In this case, we might have a two-tailed hypothesis (also known as a non-directional hypothesis) where we think something will happen, but we don’t want to specify exactly what we think will happen:

There will be a relationship between job satisfaction and job performance.

Children who watch violent media will behave with a different level of aggression compared to children who do not watch violent media.

Although we don’t mention it specifically most of the time, we are also establishing what we call a null hypothesis. We don’t mention it because it’s incredibly boring! All the null hypothesis says is that nothing is happening at all:

There will be no relationship between job satisfaction and job performance.

Children who watch violent media will have the same level of aggression compared to children who do not watch violent media.

What it is important here is that you should establish a clear hypothesis before you collect data so that you have a plan for how you will analyze the data and what the results mean for your theory.

5.3 Possible outcomes of a hypothesis test

So, once we have our hypothesis, and we know how we are going to measure our variables, we are ready to collect our data. Once we have our data, we can use it to help us infer what is going on with the population. Ultimately, when we conduct a statistical test, we actually are comparing our findings to what we would expect to find if our null hypothesis is true. In other words, we are asking the question:

What is the probability I would obtain data that looks like this, given the null hypothesis is true?

(Aha! There’s one of those conditional probabilities I mentioned in Section 4.5!)

Most of the time, we are hoping for a low probability – in most social sciences, we have set our standard at a 5% chance or lower – this is why you often see \(p<.05\) all over the place in articles. If you are curious about why 5% seems to be the magic number, the short version is “We didn’t have computers back then and calculating this probability for every statistical test was a pain.” You can read a longer version of the history of it in (Cowles and Davis 1982). The main point here is that once we think there is less than a 5% chance of us making a mistake, we say that our finding is “statistically significant.” This means that we can say that we “fail to retain the null hypothesis.”

You might be confused as to why we’re so focused on the null hypothesis instead of the other, more interesting hypothesis. It is really confusing! So let me take a moment to emphasize what we are not asking, and walk through how all this relates to Dataland and Theoryland.

A question people often incorrectly think we are asking is “What is the probability my hypothesis is true given the data I’ve obtained?” This question seems very similar to the bold question I put above, but these are actually very different questions. So, let me walk through an example to make this difference clear.

Let’s return to the violent media and aggression example. If I am an all-knowing being living in Theoryland, and I have access to all humans past and present, I have access to The Truth about whether exposure to violent media is related to aggression. Conversely, if I am a normal human living in Dataland, I simply don’t have access to all that information; I only have access to my sample that I’ve tested. So, if we think about what I know here in Dataland, and what is actually happening in Theoryland, there are four possible outcomes when I test a hypothesis asking whether there’s a difference in aggression based on whether a child is exposed to violent media:

Two outcomes are good news. If my data shows there’s a difference, and over in Theoryland, the all-knowing being looks at all the data ever and says “Yep, there is indeed a difference” then I’ve made the right call. Likewise, if my data shows no difference, and the all-knowing being says “Yup, nothing to see here!” then I’ve also made the correct decision.

However, there are also two mistakes I might make. My data might show a difference between groups, so I think that there’s a difference, but the all-knowing being sees that actually, there is no difference if I was able to test all data ever. In that case, I’m saying there’s an effect when there is not. This mistake is known as a false positive or a Type I error. If I do that, I might end up wasting all kinds of time and money studying a topic that actually doesn’t matter.

Alternatively, I might see there is absolutely no difference between groups, but the all-knowing being sees that actually, there are differences between these groups. This is known as a false negative, or a Type II error. If I make this error, I decide these variables aren’t useful, and I move on to other topics when perhaps I should have continued to research this topic.

The problem is that we don’t have any access to Theoryland, so we will never know whether we have made an error. We are always at risk of getting a funky sample or measuring a variable incorrectly, so we have to tolerate some level of uncertainty in social science. Most of the time, we are content to risk a 5% chance of making a Type I error. In some cases, perhaps a false positive is really consequential– maybe if finding an effect of violent media on children means we have to gather up all the televisions and videogames and shoot them into the sun, I’d want to make darn sure I have an accurate answer. In that case, we can be more stringent, and maybe only tolerate a 1% chance \((p<.01)\) or a .01% chance \((p<.001)\) of making a Type I error. But we can never have a perfect zero chance of making an error; errors are just part of social science.

You might be asking why I’m totally ignoring Type II errors. We’ll dig into that in the next section!

5.4 Power

In most social sciences, we don’t typically spend much time talking specifically about Type II errors – instead, you might have heard of something known as power. Power is our ability to detect a difference if one exists, and it is calculated as \(1-\beta\) (remember that \(\beta\) is our probability of a Type II error). In general, we aim to have at power of at least .80 (or put another way, we’d like our Type II error probability to be under 20%). In other words, when our study has a lot of power, it is more likely we’ll find an effect. Ideally, we want power to be as high as possible, and there are several things that can help increase power. Some of them we have some control over, and some of them we don’t.

5.4.0.1 Probability of a Type I Error

One way we can increase our chances of finding a significant finding is to increase our chances of making a Type I error. After all, if we’re less stringent about how big a difference needs to be for us to consider it present, we’re more likely to find something. You can think of \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) like on opposite ends of a see-saw – as one goes up, the other goes down. So, if we choose to set \(\alpha=.10\) instead of the traditional \(\alpha=.05\), this means we need a smaller difference between groups for us to say that we feel confident there is a difference. However, it means that we are also more at risk of making a mistake about what is important. While we certainly can do this, most journals and researchers expect you to justify why you’re deviating from the traditional threshold, and saying “Because I want to find a significant effect” is not a compelling argument. So, this is often not a practical solution for us to increase power.

5.4.0.2 The Test We Use

Another way we might be able to increase power is through the design if our research. Some tests, such as a independent t-test, compare two separate groups to one another. This is known as a between-subject test. Other tests allow us to test the same participants several times to see if an intervention changes their responses. This is known as a within-subject test. Within-subject tests are more powerful for detecting effects. Because we are looking for the change within a person instead of differences between groups, we can disregard some of the differences between individuals in a way we can’t if we are doing a between-subject test (we’ll dig into the details of this situation once we start talking about repeated t-tests in Section 7.4).

As an example for our violent media study, I’ve presented it as a between-subject test, where some children watch a violent show and others don’t. However, I could also easily set this up as a within-subject test – maybe on separate days I show the same kids different cartoons and observe them. I’d need to be careful to counterbalance my study (some kids are randomly assigned to see the non-violent media first, other kids are randomly assigned to see the violent media first) but doing this provides me with greater power to detect an effect. So, it seems ideal to always use a within-subject design, right? It doesn’t always work out that way!

In my example, I might have a hard time getting access to these children for two separate days, so it might not be realistic for me to use a within-subject design. Sometimes within-subject tests are simply not possible. If I wanted to teach a skill to people using a video versus text, I can’t re-test their ability to learn twice. As soon as they’ve learned the skill, they can’t reasonably “unlearn” it for me to test them with the opposite learning condition. So, sometimes a more powerful test is not possible for us to use, so we have limited control over this aspect of power.

5.4.0.3 The Effect Size

Sometimes, particularly in experiments, we may have some control over the size of the effect in our study. In other words, how different is the control condition from the experimental condition? In our violent media example, the control condition is non-violent media (watching characters cooperate) and the experimental condition is violent (watching characters fight). But as experimenters, we have control over just how violent the experimental condition is. If the experimental condition was only a little different from the control group (e.g., the characters get in a verbal argument and push each other) then I’m producing a small effect. For a moderate effect, I could select more aggressive behavior (e.g., a fistfight). Or I could induce a large effect by having my experimental condition by showing the children some extreme violence (a character slices dozens of other characters in half with a sword). But I also have to consider what is realistic – it’s not super likely that little kids are watching “Kill Bill” on a regular basis, so a low-to-moderate level of violence is more realistic.

If we are not doing an experiment, we don’t have any control over the size of the effect – it is what it is! So, we are not always able to increase effect size to have more power in our study.

5.4.0.4 Sample Size

Usually, the easiest way for us to increase power in a study is to obtain a larger sample size. As we learned in Chapter 3, larger samples mean we have a smaller sampling error, which means our estimates of parameters are more stable and trustworthy. When these estimates are more stable, we can feel confident that a smaller difference is meaningful and real (i.e., not due to chance). So, this is one reason why researchers like to have a decent sample size for their studies – they are more likely to be able to correctly detect differences between groups.

If this is making sense so far, you might have a really important question to ask here: “If having a large sample makes it more likely your result will be significant, can’t you just get a huge sample to ensure you get significant results?”

Well…yes, actually that is how it works! When I was learning statistics for the first time, this fact blew my mind. But this is exactly how it works – if I get a sample size of millions of people, I can find a difference of 0.0000000001 to be statistically significant. So, it’s worth spending some time here talking about what statistical significance means, what it does not, and some alternative ways of thinking about it.

5.5 Statistical Significance versus Practical Significance

As a reminder from earlier in this chapter, when we find a result is significant, it means we are saying “There is only a 5% chance that we would have data that looks like this given the null hypothesis is true” (assuming we are setting \(\alpha=.05\)).

What we are not saying is “This effect is important.” If we have a small sample, we might find a very large and important effect isn’t statistically significant, and if we have a giant sample, we might find a very tiny effect is significant. So, I want to make sure we separate out the concept of “important” and “significant.”

All we are saying when we say something is “statistically significant” is that we feel pretty certain that our findings are not due to chance; we think that something is going on out there in Theoryland.

One way we might think of importance is something called practical significance (sometimes called “clinical significance” in healthcare settings). Practical significance is whether the finding matters to real people in the real world.

Let’s return to our violent media example. Let’s say that the way I’m measuring aggression is seeing how many times a child hits another child on the playground over the course of a week. Imagine I collect a large sample, and I find that the difference between groups is statistically significant. Great! But once I look at the data, I realize that the difference between kids who do versus do not watch violent media is that they hit their friends .03 fewer times this week. It’s a LOT of work to prevent kids from seeing violent media, and all that work means that a kid hits others a tiny bit less? WHO CARES? Even though the result is statistically significant, it doesn’t really have much practical effect.

On the flip side, if I do the study with a small sample, I don’t have much power in my study so I find in the end that I don’t see a statistically significant difference between groups. SAD! But when I examine the averages for my groups, perhaps I learn that the group that watches violent media is hitting other kids 20 times more than the non-violent media group every week. It might not be statistically significant (so, I could just see this big difference due to chance) but dealing with one kid hitting another 20 times during a week is a big deal for the teachers. So, even though this study didn’t find statistically significant results, it has practical significance, so maybe I will rethink what kinds of media I show kids because it does seem to have an impact on how they behave, even though there’s a chance my findings are just an anomaly and if I conducted my study again, I might not see such a big difference.

Statistical significance is a black-or-white situation. Something either is or is not significant; saying something is “slightly significant” is like saying someone is “slightly pregnant” or “slightly dead.” It doesn’t make sense. Practical significance, meanwhile, is a judgment call, so some people might think something is practically significant and others would not.

If I have a drug that improves symptoms for 40% of people, but makes symptoms worse for 20% of people, is it helpful? If I have a training that improves test performance by 5 points, but it costs $500 per student, is it worth it? The answer depends on who you’re talking to, and whether it seems like these outcomes are valuable. That’s what practical significance is.

5.6 An Alternative to Null Hypothesis Tests

The way we’ve been talking about hypothesis testing so far is known as “null hypothesis significance testing” (NHST), sometimes known as the “frequentist approach” – we rely on p-values (the probability of a Type I error) to tell us whether something matters. This is currently the most popular way of thinking about results, but it’s not the only way!

The “Bayesian approach” is one alternative that has been gaining support slowly over the years. Rather than having a black-or-white split between “significant” and “not significant,” Bayesian approaches allow for more nuance. For example, instead of talking about statistical significance, instead we would explain to our audience how certain we are of a particular result, and what real-world implications exist for our findings (e.g., “Watching violent media was associated with children hitting other children 5-10 more times per week”).

Another way we can get away from NHST is to report effect sizes. These are often reported as r (essentially a correlation coefficient that can range from -1.00 to +1.00) or using a statistic known as Cohen’s d (which essentially describes how many standard deviations different groups are – if the effect size in our study was d=1.00, it would mean that the means for the control and experimental group are one standard deviation apart).

Even though I tend to prefer the Bayesian approach because I think it’s more transparent, the downside is that the results are not clean cut. How large of an effect is “important”? Bayesian approaches don’t specify this, so it’s up to reader interpretation. This does make it harder for us to be clear about which results matter and which results don’t matter. I won’t say much throughout the rest of this book about this approach because it’s still not the standard, but I do like students to be aware of it in case they come across Bayesian statistics in the wild.
